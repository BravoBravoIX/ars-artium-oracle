**Abstract.** *Ars artium sive ars magna cabalistica*, ascribed to Hartmann Schopper and dated 1564/1569 but surviving in 17th- and 18th-century copies, teaches a Latin question-answering machine. A question's letters become numbers, the numbers pass through nine written stages (difference lines, roots, two rhomboids, an orbit, a golden number, a cipher grid, algebra and proportion tables), and the remainders of the result after division by 23 are read as the letters of the answer. We transcribed the rules and the worked example sheets of Penn LJS 251, compared the sheets with Penn Ms. Codex 1201, and coded the first seven stages exactly as written, with every ambiguity as a declared variant. Tests and controls were committed before scoring. Fed the sheets' own inputs stage by stage, the rules reproduce the book's rhomboids (80/80 cells), branches (16/16), golden numbers (6/6) and algebra exactly, and its 108-cell cipher grids in 103/108 (sheet B) and 70/108 (sheet D) cells under the sheets' reading. Two systematic slips on sheet D (found after scoring) account for all but one of its other misses. Shuffled controls never come close (p = 0.0001). The machine is highly sensitive to the question: one changed letter alters a root in 98–99 % of cases. Yet the two printed answers are Latin verse, ordinary Latin far beyond shuffled and machine-like controls (p = 0.0001 each), and they name events after the book's own date: Ghislieri's election as Pius V in 1566, and Maximilian II's death in the 13th year of his reign in 1576. The final division that would produce the letters is ruled and left blank on both answer sheets in both copies, and the text's one worked letter count has three wrong sums and gives 42 letters for a 35-letter answer. The evidence weighs strongly toward a real, carefully followed procedure whose answers were written first.

**Results in brief**
- Stages 1–2: word sums 48/48, difference lines 154/154, last numbers 5/6, roots 20/26. The misses include two places where the rule chapter and the example sheet disagree with each other.
- Stages 3–5: rhomboids 80/80, branches 16/16, golden number 6/6 (cross rule applied; 1/6 without it). Canal roots are reduced by 24 on both sheets (18/18), not by the text's 23 (14/18).
- Stage 6, the cipher grid (108 cells): sheet B 83 (text's reading) and 103 (sheets' reading); sheet D 60 and 70, and 107 with two sheet-D slips (exploratory). Shuffle controls p = 0.0001 (Holm 0.0004); the other sheet's inputs give 6–8 cells.
- Stage 7, algebra: exact on both sheets and in the text's worked addition.
- Answers: Latin-likeness −2.06 and −2.29 nats per letter against shuffles at −3.35 and machine-like strings at −3.24 (p = 0.0001 each). Both answers name events after the book's own date.
- The division and answer stages are not worked on any sheet. The text's worked letter count (42) does not match its answer (35).

**Limits.** Two of the six known copies were read. Penn Ms. Codex 1636 could not be obtained, and the Wellcome, Florence and 2011 sale copies are not online. The transcription was made by a language model (Claude), with every tested number checked against the page images by Claude; it awaits a palaeographer's reading. Stages 3–7 are coded for 7–10-word questions only. The division and answer stages (chapters VIII–X) are not implemented, because the worked division cannot be followed from the text and the sheets leave it blank. The literature on number-"cabala" and lottery oracles (Stigler 2003; Macry 1997; the Benincasa tradition) and Mundhenk's Killy article on Schopper were not read.
