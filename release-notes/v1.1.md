**What changed in 1.1.** The machine now runs end to end from the question (`code/machine.py`). Five new pre-registered tests (12–16) were run once. Every departure of the book from its own rules is listed with its correction. Two missing leaves of LJS 251 were identified. Earlier results are unchanged.

**Abstract.** *Ars artium sive ars magna cabalistica*, ascribed to Hartmann Schopper and dated 1564/1569 but surviving in 17th- and 18th-century copies, teaches a Latin question-answering machine whose printed answers are Latin verse. Version 1.0 rebuilt its first seven stages stage by stage. Version 1.1 chains them, so the machine runs from the question alone. It adds the algebra built from the roots, the division where the text gives it in full (golden number 0 or 1), and the rules that turn the quotients into a starting cell, a first letter and a letter count. The tests and controls were committed before scoring. From the question alone, the machine reproduces about two thirds of every printed value on the three example sheets (130/192, 83/126, 133/192). Random questions average 7–17 matches (p = 0.0001 each), so the sheets were really computed from their questions, up to the cipher grid. The answers cannot be reached. Both answered questions need the general division (golden number 8 by the rules, 5 on the sheet; and 2). Its rule ran onto pp. 85–86 of LJS 251, which are missing, as are pp. 105–106 with the first proportion table, which about half of all cipher cells would need. The book's 22 departures from its own rules, each listed with its correction, are mostly copying and arithmetic slips. They do not cluster where the answer is decided: 7 of 50 sheet departures fall among the 54 values that feed the division, against 7.0 by chance (p = 0.58). The text's only complete worked letter count uses a golden number of 0, which the rules do not give for that question. Corrected, it gives 46 letters; the book says 42 and the printed answer has 35. The evidence weighs strongly toward a real procedure whose answers were written first. No route in the surviving rules leads from either question to its verse.

**Results in brief**
- Test 12, chained reproduction: B 130/192, C 83/126, D 133/192 printed values from the question alone. 10,000 random questions average 13.8, 7.2 and 16.9 (best 38, 30, 50); p = 0.0001 each, Holm 0.0003. Front, rhomboids and branches exact; each wrong root carries forward.
- Test 13, departures: 50 on the sheets (5 roots, one residue sign, one cipher line of 5 cells, sheet D's 38-cell slips) and 14 in the text's worked algebra. That makes 22 items, each with its correction. Both text additions leave out root 7. The corrected Maximilian count is 46 letters.
- Test 14, clustering: 7 of 50 departures in the answer path, 7.03 expected, p = 0.58 (smallest possible p 1.6 × 10⁻⁵⁸).
- Test 15, the answers: golden number 8 (B) and 2 (D). The surviving rules cannot carry either past the division. Random questions reach a letter count in 3.4 % of cases, and those counts are unusable (median 224, a quarter zero or negative).
- Test 16: 49 % of cipher cells need the missing multiple table.
- Exploratory: one pattern break in each surviving table (229 for 219; 23 for 24).

**Limits.** Part 1 was read in one copy (LJS 251). The lost pages may survive in Amsterdam BPH PH337, which was not consulted for them. Part 2 of LJS 251 was not transcribed. The machine is coded for 9-word questions. The text's worked numbers were read again from the images by Claude (model Opus 5.5, in Claude Code), not by a person; the arithmetic checks are by code. The rule readings marked as variants were chosen in advance.
